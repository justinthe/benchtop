# How spectacles work

Some people can't see far things sharply, and some can't see near things. Glasses fix this by bending light a
little before it enters the eye. I want people to see **why**: an eye model where light from a distant object is
focused by the lens, and the picture only comes out sharp when the focus lands exactly on the retina.

Let people change the **eye length** (a longer eye is short-sighted, a shorter eye is long-sighted) and put a
**lens** in front of the eye (concave or convex, any power in dioptres) and watch the rays and the focus point
move. Show where the focus lands, how blurry the picture on the retina gets, and what lens power would fix it.

Simple reduced-eye model is fine: one focusing surface with fixed power of about 60 dioptres and the eye filled
with fluid of index 1.336, so the eye focuses distant light at 1336/60 = 22.3 mm behind the lens. A normal eye is
that long. Pupil about 4 mm.

Ideas for guided modes: a normal eye; a short-sighted eye (long, say 24.5 mm); the same eye corrected with a concave
lens; a long-sighted eye (short, say 21 mm) corrected with a convex lens.

Worked numbers to honour:
- Needed lens power in dioptres = 1336 / eye length (mm) − 60, so that a 24.5 mm eye needs about −5.5 D and a 21.0 mm
  eye about +3.6 D. A 22.3 mm eye needs 0.
- A longer eye means about 2.7 dioptres per millimetre of error.
- Ignore the small gap between glasses and eye.

Show: a diegetic screen on the bench with what the retina sees, sharp or blurred (a letter chart is a nice picture),
and live numbers for the focus position, the blur size and the correction needed. Put the eye model on an optics bench
in a bright room, like the prism one.

Out of scope: astigmatism, ageing and reading glasses, contact lenses, cataracts.
